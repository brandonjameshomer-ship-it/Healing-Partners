# Narrowing — step 4

**This is the essential step.** Everything else supports it.

The problem the product exists to solve is not that families lack options. It is that they have
too many, at the worst possible moment, and cannot choose between them. A thick catalogue is not a
service — it is the source of the paralysis. Steps 2 and 3 gather and generate; **step 4 is where
the burden actually comes off the family.** If narrowing is done badly, the product has failed
even if every other step went well.

Target: **three to five elements.**

---

## Why three to five

Below three, the memorial is generic. Above five, it is cluttered — the stone has finite space,
and every element competes with the others for the eye. Five is roughly the point where a passer-by
stops reading a stone as a portrait and starts reading it as a list.

---

## If they can already choose

Many families can. Ask which ones matter most and accept the answer. Do not run a process for its
own sake — that adds burden rather than removing it. Move to step 5.

---

## If they cannot — pairwise comparison

People who cannot rank seven things can nearly always choose between two. That asymmetry is the
whole mechanism.

**How to run it:**

1. Take everything they flagged as mattering.
2. Present **exactly two**, side by side, with the reason each was suggested.
3. Ask: **"Which of these two matters more?"**
4. Keep the winner, set the other aside.
5. Repeat until three to five remain.

Show the rationale each time, not just the name. Not "Bronze medallion or granite bench?" but:

> **A bronze medallion of an open book and reading glasses** — for the lending library on her porch
>
> **or**
>
> **A granite bench** — somewhere for people to sit and stay a while

The reason is what they are actually choosing between. Stripped of it, they are picking objects.
With it, they are picking which part of her the stone should carry.

---

## Handling what comes up

**"Both, equally."** Allow it. Keep both and continue. Forcing a false choice damages trust. If
they say this to everything, they can probably keep more than five — ask whether the design should
be larger, or split across a companion stone.

**"Neither."** Useful information. Drop both and note the whole direction may be wrong. If it keeps
happening, return to step 2 — you did not get enough.

**The family disagrees with each other.** Common, and often the hardest moment in the whole process.
Do not adjudicate. Say plainly that both are worth keeping and continue — the comparison will
usually resolve it on its own a round or two later. If it does not, ask **whose memorial decision
this is**, gently. Someone is usually the designated decision-maker.

**They want to go back.** Always allow it. Nothing is locked until step 7.

**They get upset.** Stop the process. Sit with it. The comparison can wait. You are not running a
survey.

---

## Keep it short

Do not run more comparisons than needed. Going from eight items to five takes three comparisons,
not twenty-eight. Every extra question spends attention this family does not have.

If you find yourself past six or seven comparisons, stop and ask directly which ones they want to
keep. The mechanism exists to reduce burden; when it stops doing that, abandon it.

---

## Closing the step

Read the final list back with the reason attached to each:

> "So: the rose vine border for the Peace roses, the bronze book medallion for the porch library,
> and her own words for the epitaph. Three things. Does that feel like her?"

**"Does that feel like her"** is the question that matters. If they hesitate, go back. A yes here is
what makes the three proofs in step 5 land — they are no longer evaluating designs in the abstract,
they are seeing something they already agreed was true.
