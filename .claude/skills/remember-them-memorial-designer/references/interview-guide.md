# Interview guide

The bar: **a stranger walking past the finished memorial should understand who this person was.**

That is a high bar, and you will not clear it by asking whether they were kind. You clear it by
getting one concrete, specific thing — the porch lending library, the candy-apple Mustang in the
Fourth of July parade, the cameo brooch that came from her mother.

---

## How good interviewers do it

What Terkel, Gross, and Isay have in common, and what to borrow:

**Ask about behaviour, not character.** "What did their hands look like?" beats "Were they
hard-working?" Adjectives produce adjectives; specifics produce stories.

**Ask about the ordinary.** "What would they be doing on an ordinary Saturday?" gets you the
real texture of a life. Milestones are generic — everyone graduates.

**Follow the energy.** When their voice changes, stop and stay there. That is the memorial.

**Let silence do the work.** People fill pauses with the thing they were deciding whether to say.
Do not rescue them from a silence.

**Ask one question at a time.** Stacked questions get you an answer to the easiest one.

**Ask the question they are waiting for.** "What would embarrass them to hear us say?" gives
permission to be honest, which is often a relief.

---

## Opening

Start wide and unstructured:

> **"Tell me about them. However it comes out — a story, a list, the thing you'd say at the
> service. There's no wrong way, and you can't tell me too much."**

Do not interrupt. Do not take them somewhere else. Whatever they choose to say first is what
matters most to them.

---

## Follow-ups

Pick three or four based on what they gave you. Do not run this as a checklist.

**Work and craft**
- What did their hands look like?
- What did they make, build, or grow that's still standing?
- What did people come to them for?

**Family and home**
- Who did they take care of?
- What did the house sound like when everyone was there?
- What did they always say?

**Faith** *(only if they raised it)*
- Where did they sit in church?
- What did their faith look like on an ordinary Tuesday?

**Outdoors and place**
- Where were they happiest outside?
- What did they know about that place that nobody else did?

**Music and art**
- What would be playing?
- What did they sing when they thought no one could hear?

**Service** *(ask directly — never assume)*
- Did they serve? *(Ask plainly. Do not infer it from an interest in war history.)*
- What did they never talk about?

**Humour**
- What would make them laugh until they couldn't breathe?

**Closing**
- What would embarrass them to hear us say about them?
- If someone who never met them walked past this stone, what should they understand?

---

## Second and third passes

After the first round, reflect back what you heard and ask what is missing. People remember more
once they hear their own words. A second pass is normal; a third is fine.

---

## When the family struggles

Grief makes recall hard. That is not a failure.

- Offer a menu instead of an open question — "was he more of a fixer, a talker, or a quiet one?"
- Ask about objects. "What's still in the garage?" Objects carry stories when memory stalls.
- Ask about other people. "Who's going to miss him most, and why?"
- Let them stop. Say you have enough to start and can add more later. You almost always do.

---

## Turning answers into design

Work from specific to visual. Not "she was religious" → cross. Rather:

> "She kept a shelf of books on the porch rail for the neighbourhood"
> → a bronze medallion of an open book with reading glasses.

The test: could someone who never met her look at the finished stone and get *something* true
about her? If the element would fit any stranger equally well, it is not carrying its weight.

Always name the connection out loud when you propose it. "A rose vine border, because of the
Peace roses along her fence." That sentence is what makes them trust the design.
